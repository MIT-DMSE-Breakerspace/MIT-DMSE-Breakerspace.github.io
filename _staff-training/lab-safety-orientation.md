# Lab Safety Orientation

Last reviewed: 2026-07-31

A short in-person orientation to the physical lab, delivered at the start of every instrument training. It covers the things a web page cannot: where the equipment actually is, and what to do standing in the room.

This is the canonical version. Instrument guides reference it and add only instrument-specific safety content; they should not restate these steps.

## Why This Exists

The [public safety page]({% link safety.md %}) is thorough, and the one-time Qualtrics record has trainees review and acknowledge it. But reading a rule and being able to act on it in the room are different.

As of 2026-07-31, `safety.md` publishes every emergency-equipment location: the sink-area first-aid cabinet, shower, eyewash, spill kit, and extinguisher; the campus phone on the desk by the lab doors; and the fact that alarm pulls are in the main corridor rather than in the lab. So this orientation is no longer the sole source for any of it.

It is still worth five minutes, for three reasons that a published page cannot cover:

* **Pointing at a thing is different from reading its description.** "On the desk by the lab doors" is unambiguous only once you have seen which desk.
* **Two rules are counterintuitive and easy to get backwards under stress.** The campus phone is inside the lab, so it is the wrong tool during a fire evacuation. Alarm pulls are deliberately *not* in the lab. Both are worth saying out loud rather than leaving to a reader.
* **No safety walk is required of anyone.** The safety requirement in the training pathway is satisfied by a web page and a checkbox. This is the in-person counterpart that was missing.

## Run It Every Session, For Everyone

Deliver the orientation at the start of every training, regardless of who is registered.

The reasoning, since the alternative is tempting:

* **A trainer cannot reliably tell who is new.** LibCal gives a registration list, not a training history. Checking whether all three participants have trained before costs more than delivering the orientation.
* **Sessions hold up to three people.** If even one has not been in the lab before, the orientation happens anyway. All-returning sessions are the exception.
* **"Optional" becomes "skipped."** An optional first item in a 60-minute session with a hands-on exercise will be dropped under time pressure, and the sessions where it is dropped are not the sessions where it was safe to drop it.
* **The room changes.** Equipment gets relocated, and the lounge configuration is an unresolved question in the site to-do. A returning user's mental map may be out of date.
* **Repetition is the point for emergency information.** Nobody looks up an exit route during a fire.

**Scale it, do not skip it.** When everyone present has clearly worked in the lab before, a 60-second confirmation is enough: point out the exit, the eyewash and shower, and the campus phone, and ask whether anything has moved since they were last in. When anyone is new, walk the full version. The floor is never zero.

## The Walk

Do this on foot, pointing at each thing. A list read aloud at the instrument does not accomplish what walking to the sink does.

Keep it to about five minutes. It is an orientation, not a safety course — `safety.md` and the Qualtrics record carry the full rules.

Weight the time toward what is counterintuitive or gets used: the single exit route, the campus phone's location and its fire-evacuation exception, that alarm pulls are in the corridor rather than the lab, sharps disposal, and the glove transition. The sink-area fixtures are published and, in the case of the shower and eyewash, should never be needed under the lab's material rules.

### 1. Exit Route

Walk it, or at minimum point along its full length. **The lab has one exit route:** through the lab door into the lounge, then through the lounge door into the Building 8 corridor, then to the nearest building exit. No elevators.

Say that it is a single route, and that keeping it clear matters for that reason.

### 2. Emergency Equipment At The Sink

Most of it is in one place, which makes this quick:

* **Emergency and first-aid supplies** — the labeled cabinet above the sink. This is the item most likely to be used, for an ordinary cut or scrape.
* **Spill kit** — the same sink area. Note that it is for a minor, known spill only; anything unknown, large, or hazardous means leave and get help.
* **Emergency shower and eyewash** — immediately to the left of the sink. Point them out and say that eyes or skin means fifteen minutes of flushing, not a few seconds, since stopping early is the common mistake.

Point out that access to all of it must stay clear.

#### On The Shower And Eyewash

Keep this brief and do not build the session around it. If the material rules are being followed, the shower and eyewash should never be needed here.

They are not present because this lab's hazards require them. MIT policy adds a shower and eyewash to any lab under renovation so the fixtures are already in place if that space's use later changes, and that is why these exist. **From an EHS standpoint, the Breakerspace's current non-hazardous material restrictions do not require this to be covered in training.**

Two reasons to spend fifteen seconds on it anyway:

* Isopropyl alcohol is the routine ATR-crystal cleaning solvent and is handled during FTIR sessions. It is non-hazardous by the lab's standard and is not a reason for alarm, but it is a real liquid going near someone's hands and face, and knowing where to flush is cheap.
* Users who go on to other MIT labs will meet the same fixtures under stricter material rules. A correct first exposure transfers.

What to avoid: implying the Breakerspace handles materials that need emergency flushing. That misrepresents the lab and undercuts the actual rule, which is that hazardous materials do not come in without prior approval. **The material restriction is the safety control here; the fixtures are a building feature.** If a trainee's question suggests they are planning to bring something requiring an eyewash, that is a stop-and-ask-staff conversation, not a demonstration.


### 3. Sharps Disposal

The sharps container near the sample-preparation area. Mention **glass slides** explicitly — that is the disposal mistake most likely to happen in this lab, and slides do not go in the regular trash.

Point out the ordinary trash and recycling while you are there.

### 4. Calling For Help

* **Show the campus phone** — on the desk by the lab doors — and say to dial **100** from it.
* From a mobile phone, **617-253-1212**.
* Say explicitly: **use these numbers rather than 911** on campus, so the call reaches MIT emergency services directly.
* Have them say the room out loud: **Building 8, Room 8-102A**, entered through the Breakerspace Lounge. Under stress, the room number is the thing people cannot produce, and saying it once in a calm room makes it retrievable.

Make the one distinction that matters here: the campus phone is **inside** the lab. For an injury or a contained problem where staying is safe, use it. **For fire or smoke, do not stay in the lab or come back for it** — leave the building and call from a mobile phone outside.

### 5. Fire Alarm Pull And Extinguisher

Both are commonly misunderstood, so state the reasoning rather than only the location.

* **Alarm pulls are in the main corridor near the building exits, never inside the lab.** That is deliberate: you pass one on the way out, so using it moves you toward safety instead of back into the building. Tell participants not to look for one in the lab.
* **A fire extinguisher is to the left of the sink, near the shower and eyewash, with signage.** Point it out and immediately say that **MIT policy is not to fight a fire yourself.** The location is worth knowing; using it is not the plan.

The action is the same in both cases: get out, pull an alarm in the corridor on the way, call for help from outside.

### 6. The Food And Drink Boundary

Point at the actual line: food and drinks are fine in the lounge, not in the lab. This is easier to convey by gesture than by policy text, and it comes up constantly because people arrive with coffee from the lounge.

### 7. Gloves

Demonstrate the transition rather than describing it: gloves on for sample handling and loading, **gloves off before touching a keyboard, mouse, or touchscreen**, clean gloves back on to return to the sample.

This protects the user and the equipment, and it is the habit most often skipped once training ends. Doing it visibly here sets the expectation for the instrument portion of the session that follows.

### 8. Getting Help, And Stopping

Close with the culture point:

* Find staff in the lab; otherwise `#breakerspace-help` or the staff email.
* **Stop rather than push through** an unexpected sound, smell, warning, damaged part, or anything unfamiliar.
* Report damage, spills, injuries, and near misses **even when they seem resolved**, and say directly that reporting exists to fix conditions, not to blame the person who asked for help. Trainees who believe otherwise hide problems.
* Mention that a user may end up in the lab alone, and that unfamiliar or higher-risk work is a reason to ask staff rather than proceed.

## Fitting It Into 60 Minutes

The orientation takes about five minutes of a fixed 60-minute session, so something else compresses. Take it from the instrument-overview discussion rather than from cleanup, shutdown, or closeout — those are the parts with consequences that outlast the session.

If a particular instrument's full workflow genuinely cannot fit alongside the orientation, that is a scheduling decision to raise with the lab manager, not a reason to rush the end of the session. Do not solve a timing problem by skipping shutdown.

## What Stays In The Instrument Guide

This orientation covers the room. Each instrument guide still covers its own:

* Instrument-specific PPE beyond gloves, such as safety glasses for the Instron and hardness tester.
* Sample compatibility limits and what requires staff review.
* Instrument-specific stop-and-ask conditions.
* Hazards particular to that instrument, such as X-ray enclosures and interlocks, laser safety, moving crossheads and stored energy, or vacuum and cold-stage behavior.
